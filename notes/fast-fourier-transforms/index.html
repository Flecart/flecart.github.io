Fourier Transform and Fast Multiplication#

When to Reach for Fourier#

Here we revise when Fourier is the right tool, before how it works. The thing you have to remember: the test is about the system, not the signal.

The LTI Trigger#

Fourier diagonalises every operator that is linear and shift-invariant (LTI). Complex exponentials are its eigenfunctions.

  • Linear: T(αx+βy)=αT(x)+βT(y).
  • Shift-invariant: T commutes with the shift Sτ​, i.e. T(Sτ​x)=Sτ​T(x).
  • Consequence: T(eiωt)=H(ω)eiωt. A sine goes in and the same sine comes out, only scaled and phase-shifted.

Why: the eigenvectors of the shift are exponentials, and an operator that commutes with the shift shares its eigenvectors (see Simultaneous Diagonalisation). Any LTI operator is a Convolution, and in the Fourier basis convolution becomes pointwise multiplication.

Ask about the system, not the signal

A one-off event (a clap, a drum strike) can still be analysed with Fourier if the medium it passes through is LTI. "Independent" means the system treats each frequency separately, not that the input's components are unrelated.

Surface cues

Blurring, smoothing, echo, diffusion, "each output is a weighted average of its neighbours", periodic forcing, and any sum whose indices add up (∑i+j=k​).

Anti-Triggers#

  • Localised in time or space (transients, edges) → Short-Time Fourier Transform, Wavelets. Uncertainty principle: narrow in time means broad in frequency.
  • Nonlinear systems create new frequencies (harmonics), so no clean per-frequency description exists.
  • Non-stationary rules (the system changes over time) break shift-invariance.

Convolution in Disguise#

Multiplication Is Convolution#

Write digits units-first, so position = power of 10: a=∑i​ai​10i. The core fact is that exponents add when you multiply.

a⋅bck​​=i∑​j∑​ai​bj​10i+j=k∑​ck​10k,=i+j=k∑​ai​bj​=i∑​ai​bk−i​(convolution)​

Example: 123×45, so a=[3,2,1] and b=[5,4]:

j=0 (5)j=1 (4)
i=0 (3)15 → k=012 → k=1
i=1 (2)10 → k=18 → k=2
i=2 (1)5 → k=24 → k=3

Summing the anti-diagonals gives c=[15,22,13,4]. Carrying then gives 5535.

Schoolbook multiplication without carrying shows exactly the convolution:


          1    2    3
      ×        4    5
          5   10   15
     4    8   12
     4   13   22   15   ← c3 c2 c1 c0
  • Carrying is separate: it uses floor and mod, so it is nonlinear. Do it once, after the convolution.
  • Shifted copies: a⋅b=∑j​bj​Sja. This is a weighted sum of shifted copies of a, structurally identical to a blur kernel.

Origin of the flip

i+j=k: a step forward in one sequence is a step backward in the other. Reading order is irrelevant; only the relative flip matters.

Circular Convolution and Padding#

The DFT indexes modulo N, so it computes circular convolution: high positions wrap onto low ones. With N=3, c3​ wraps into c0​, giving 15+4=19 ✗.

Pad to N≥len(a)+len(b)−1.

Same phenomenon as zero-padding vs circular padding in CNNs.

Other Disguised Convolutions#

  • Polynomial multiplication (coefficients).
  • PMF of a sum of independent random variables → Characteristic Function.
  • Moving averages, discretised diffusion.
  • Wildcard string matching via FFT.

From Convolution to Pointwise Product#

Evaluate → Multiply → Interpolate#

Replace 10 by x: A(x)=3+2x+x2, B(x)=5+4x, and C(x)=A(x)B(x) for every x.

  • x=2: A=11, B=13, C=143=11⋅13.
  • x=−1: A=2, B=1, C=2.

At a single point, the whole convolution collapses to one scalar multiplication.

How many points: degC+1=len(a)+len(b)−1. Two distinct polynomials of degree ≤d agreeing on d+1 points would have a nonzero difference of degree ≤d with d+1 roots, which is impossible.

Worked interpolation with points {0,1,−1,2}, C-values {15,54,2,143}:

c0​C(1)+C(−1)C(1)−C(−1)C(2)​=C(0)=15=2c0​+2c2​=56⇒c2​=13=2c1​+2c3​=52=15+2c1​+52+8c3​=143⇒c3​=4, c1​=22​

The general move

Switch to a representation where the hard operation becomes easy, provided switching is cheap. With arbitrary points, evaluation and interpolation cost O(n2), so nothing is gained.

Link to Karatsuba and Toom–Cook#

Evaluating at small points is itself a real algorithm family. Karatsuba Algorithm: split into halves (degree-1 polynomials), evaluate at {0,1,∞}, so 3 products instead of 4, giving O(n1.585). Toom–3: points {0,1,−1,−2,∞}. The FFT is the limit of this idea, with N points chosen so the transform itself is fast.

The FFT#

Even/Odd Split#

A(x)=E(x2)+xO(x2)

For A=[3,2,1,0]: E(y)=3+y and O(y)=2+0y=2. The x is factored out of the odd terms, so O is constant here.

Butterfly (from one pair of child values, get two parent values):

A(x)A(−x)​=E(x2)+xO(x2)=E(x2)−xO(x2)​

Going up = evaluating the nested expression from the inside out: A(x)=[3+x2⋅1]+x[2+x2⋅0]. The brackets depend only on x2, so they are shared by ±x.

Why Roots of Unity#

The regrouping holds for any x. Roots are needed so that the set of points halves at every level.

Point set (N=8)Distinct points per levelSaving
Random8 → 8 → 8None
±1,±2,±3,±48 → 4 → 4 → 4One level only (squares are positive)
8th roots of unity8 → 4 → 2 → 1Every level
Halving lemma

Squaring doubles the angle, so the N equally spaced points fold onto N/2 equally spaced points, which again form ± pairs: ωk+N/2=−ωk.

Levels and Twiddle Factors#

Points are fixed by the level, not chosen by hand (N=8, ω=e2πi/8):

LevelSub-polynomial sizePoints
01{1}
12{1,−1}
24{1,i,−1,−i}
38every 45°
  • At each level, the x values are the points in the upper half of the circle, [0°,180°), and −x is the antipode.
  • The butterfly at x=ωk needs child values at ω2k, which were computed one level below.
  • The multiplier x is the twiddle factor.

Worked Example, N=4#

A=[3,2,1,0] at {1,−1,i,−i}:

L1:L2:​E(±1)=3±1=4,2O(±1)=2±0=2,2A(±1)=4±2=6,2A(±i)=2±2i​

Complexity#

T(N)=2T(N/2)+O(N)=O(NlogN),#butterflies=2N​log2​N

For N=8: 3 levels × 4 butterflies = 12.

Inverse FFT#

Evaluation at the roots is the Vandermonde Matrix Vkj​=ωjk. Orthogonality of sampled waves:

k=0∑N−1​ωk(j−l)=Nδjl​⇒V−1=N1​V

So interpolation is the same FFT with ω−1, divided by N. With arbitrary points, inverting V is expensive and ill-conditioned.

Where the Fourier Part Is#

Evaluation at Roots = DFT#

A(ωk)=j∑​aj​e2πijk/N,eiθ=cosθ+isinθ

The powers of ωk are a sampled wave with k cycles. So A(ωk) = (sequence · probe wave), i.e. how much of frequency k is in the sequence.

  • A(1)=∑j​aj​: frequency 0 (DC, the mean).
  • A(−1)=∑j​(−1)jaj​: the highest frequency (Nyquist).

Standard DFT convention uses e−2πi: same thing, traversing the circle the other way.

Eigenvector View#

Convolution with b is a Circulant Matrix Cb​, and the DFT diagonalises it:

Cb​=F−1diag(Fb)F

The algebra explains why it's fast; the eigenvector view explains why it works for any LTI system.

Big-Integer Multiplication: Full Algorithm#

Pipeline#

  1. Digits units-first; pad to a power of 2 with N≥len(a)+len(b)−1.
  2. FFT both, multiply pointwise, inverse FFT.
  3. Round to nearest integer (removes floating-point noise).
  4. Carry, O(n).
import cmath

def fft(a, inv=False):
    n = len(a)
    if n == 1:
        return a
    E, O = fft(a[0::2], inv), fft(a[1::2], inv)   # even / odd split
    s = -1 if inv else 1
    out = [0] * n
    for k in range(n // 2):
        w = cmath.exp(s * 2j * cmath.pi * k / n)   # twiddle factor
        out[k] = E[k] + w * O[k]                   # A(x)
        out[k + n // 2] = E[k] - w * O[k]          # A(-x)
    return out

def multiply(x, y):
    a = [int(d) for d in str(x)[::-1]]             # units first
    b = [int(d) for d in str(y)[::-1]]
    n = 1
    while n < len(a) + len(b) - 1:                 # avoid wrap-around
        n *= 2
    A = fft(a + [0] * (n - len(a)))
    B = fft(b + [0] * (n - len(b)))
    c = fft([p * q for p, q in zip(A, B)], inv=True)
    c = [round((v / n).real) for v in c]           # 1/N, kill float noise
    return sum(ck * 10**k for k, ck in enumerate(c))   # carry

assert multiply(123, 45) == 5535

State of the Art#

  • Number-Theoretic Transform: replace C with Z/p (roots of unity exist mod suitable primes), so the arithmetic is exact.
  • Schönhage and Strassen's algorithm (1971): rings Z/(2m+1), where 2 is a root of unity. O(nlognloglogn).
  • Harvey and van der Hoeven's algorithm (2019): O(nlogn), conjectured optimal.
  • GMP: schoolbook → Karatsuba → Toom–Cook → FFT as the size grows.

Drawbacks#

  • Constant factors: the FFT wins only for large n.
  • Floating-point precision: rounding fails at large n or large digit bases → NTT.
  • Wrap-around if padding is forgotten.

CNN Layers Through the Fourier Lens#

Conv Layer as Per-Frequency Matrices#

Without the activation, a conv layer is LTI. In the Fourier domain:

y^​cout​​(ξ)=cin​∑​w^cout​,cin​​(ξ)x^cin​​(ξ)

So a conv layer is one Cout​×Cin​ matrix per frequency, with no mixing across frequencies.

  • Fast conv via FFT for large kernels; 3×3 kernels use Winograd instead.
  • Small kernels give blunt filters: compact support forces smooth frequency responses (uncertainty principle). Depth multiplies responses, which sharpens them.
  • Exact singular values = singular values of the per-frequency matrices → Lipschitz control (Sedghi et al., ICLR 2019).
  • Fourier Neural Operator (Anandkumar's group, ICLR 2021): learn the per-frequency weights directly; keep low modes.

Adjacent and Distant Ideas#

  • Logarithms: the original transform → cheap operation → inverse trick.
  • Probability: characteristic functions turn sums of independent RVs into products.
  • Heat equation: Fourier's original problem; each mode decays independently at a rate ∝k2.
  • Graph Fourier Transform: on a graph, Laplacian eigenvectors replace sines; this is the basis of spectral GNNs.
  • Consensus dynamics: averaging on a ring or lattice is LTI, so disagreement decomposes into modes; long-wavelength disagreement decays slowest. This is relevant when thinking about how norms spread over a lattice of agents (norm-emergence).

Fourier's heat problem — Step 1: Heat on a ring of cells#

Fourier studied how temperature evolves in a solid, including a metal ring. Start with a ring cut into cells. At each time step, every cell moves a little toward the average of its two neighbours:

u_j ← u_j + α·(u_{j−1} − 2u_j + u_{j+1})

A cell hotter than its neighbours cools down, and a colder one warms up. Written as a kernel, the update is a sliding window with weights [α, 1−2α, α]: the same kernel applied at every cell, wrapping around the ring.

Example: 4 cells, α = 0.25, starting at [4, 0, 0, 0], so all the heat is in one spot.

  • u₀ = 4 + 0.25·(0 − 8 + 0) = 2
  • u₁ = 0 + 0.25·(4 − 0 + 0) = 1
  • u₂ = 0 + 0.25·(0 − 0 + 0) = 0
  • u₃ = 0 + 0.25·(0 − 0 + 4) = 1

After one step the heat is [2, 1, 0, 1]: the peak has spread to its neighbours, and the total is still 4.

Check (two parts):

  1. Is this update linear and shift-invariant? What does that tell you, given everything above?
  2. Compute the next step from [2, 1, 0, 1].

The algorithm has been the same, some ideas are in Fourier Series, but architectures change, which means there are new ways to make this algorithm even faster.

Example of transforms#

We have learned in Algebra lineare numerica, Cambio di Base that linear transforms are usually a change of basis. They are matrix vector multiplications (additions and multiplications by constants). The optimizations are based on what sorts of transforms we have (e.g. Sparse Matrix Vector Multiplication, or dense versions). The same idea applies also for Fourier transforms.

Discrete Fourier Transforms#

Primitive roots#

It uses primitive roots of n as coefficients. Discrete Fourier transform computes:

yk​​=l=0∑n−1​xl​⋅e−2πinkl​=l=0∑n−1​xl​⋅ωkl​

Where ω=e−2πi/n is the primitive root of n.

The matrix DFTn​ is a square matrix of size n and the entries are given by:

DFTn​[k][l]=ωkl=e−2πinkl​

Example of DFT matrices#

At each index we will have the ij th unit of root. For example if n=2 we know that the root is −1, then the matrix would be:

DFT2​=(11​1−1​)

This has two adds (one add and sub), it would be quite inefficient to write the multiplication part. This is usually called butterflies in signal processing, due to the nature of the circuit.

for n=4, the primitive root is i we have:

DFT4​=​1111​1i−1−i​1−11−1​1−i−1i​​

If we assume complex everything, it has 12 additions and 4 multiplications. This matrix can be factorized, then we can add it as a series of matrix vector products.

Decomposing into sparse MVM#

If T=T1​T2​...Tk​, we can write:

t1​t2​⋮tk​y​=T1​x=T2​t1​=Tk​tk−1​=tk​​

This makes sense if Ti​ are sparse (see Sparse Matrix Vector Multiplication, and not too many m steps).

You can use Group Theory to find this kind of transformations.

The above representation can be rewritten using matrix algebra:

DFT4​=(DFT2​⊗I2​)⋅diag(1,1,1,i)⋅(I2​⊗DFT2​)L24​

You can see that it is also quite easy to implement it in hardware.

It can be similar for size 16 and radix 4.

Other transforms#

IMDCT and DCT are useful transforms for MPEG and JPEG format, while DFT and its inverse can be considered universal transformation tools.

FFT transformations#

General Radix Recursive Cooley-Tukey FFT#

This is the most general version of DFT, it has a similar structure compared to the previous ones.

DFTn​​=(DFTn/r​⊗Ir​)⋅diag(1,1,...,ω0,ω1,...,ωr−1)⋅(In/r​⊗DFTr​)Ln/rn​​

The diagonal matrix has the roots of unity. This is called the decimation in time frequency. It is possible also to use the decimation in frequency, which is just the transpose. We can decompose everything until we have size two butterfly operations (bunch of adds).

Cost of DFT#

Independently of recursion (the radix), we have a number of

  • nlog2​(n) additions and nlog2​(n)/2 multiplications, but to be real we would need to consider real additions, so we have to multiply everything by two, to get the adds. For multiplication, we have four multiplication and two additions (image it as a matrix) (in reality you use the structure of roots of unity, so in reality you have less adds). Usually best is around radix 8
  • You will have about ≤3nlog2​(n) real additions and ≤2nlog2​(n) complex multiplications, so upper bound of 5 operations. For a long time radix 2 iterative version was the best version, after superscalar processors like Skylake Microprocessor it has better versions. There is a practical lower bound analysis, but that is like Strassen Fast Linear Algebra, which is not much useful.

Recursive vs iterative FFT#

The recursive version is good for re-use, has better locality. Triple loop is like the blocked MMM in Fast Linear Algebra. If we have big stages computed, we can continue with the next stage immediately.

Iterative similar, the op count is the same, is slower because of the (log n passes through the data). The code change is quite difficult. If we have much data, then at each iteration we need to load the whole data which could be expensive.

Ideas for FFTW#

Merging Steps#

  1. In the original version, the original permutation could be very expensive, to load everything. There is no need to keep it as a sperate step, we can fuse it in the next step.
    1. This fusion would need more data, because it needs to write to another place (no-inplace computation!).
  2. The scaling can also be fused, with the previous or next step (just change the kernel).

Strides will change as we recurse for the DFTrec, and we can also interchange input and output to save space. Because of the power of two strides, often it is very difficult to reach peak performance.

Constants#

We precompute the constants of the sins and cosines to save time (just need what roots of unity you need in order to compute it). They just depend on the input size, so they precompute it at the beginning of the function.

Block un-rolling#

We un-roll the basic blocks at the end to get longer codes, less recursion (size 32 is good basecase for example).

Program Generator#

We have DAG generator (for many types of FFT), a simplifier for the DAG (specific for the FFT computation).

  • An example of a simplification is when you have x−y and y−x, an optimization would be flipping one after you have computed one.
  • Also constants come in pairs (minus and positive version).
    • Reduces register pressure.
